The brief

What you know going in

The catalog endpoint loads the list of products and then the details for each one. It's correct, and there's no error, no deploy, no resource problem. It's just slower than it should be, and the product service sees far more calls than there are catalog loads. One trace explains both at once.

Step 4 · Name the pattern

            
One call to fetch the list, then one more call for each item in it. What's this called?

            

              
              
              
            

            
This is the N+1 problem. It shows up anywhere you loop over a list and fetch each row on its own, classically with database queries behind an ORM, and just as often with service-to-service calls like these.

          

          

            The pattern
            
1 to list, N to fill in

            
The "+1" is the query that gets the list; the "N" is the one-per-item calls that follow. The waterfall's tell-tale shape is a run of identical spans, all to the same place, differing only by the id.

          

        


        
        

          

            
Step 5 · Why it matters

            
There are only a handful of items today. Why care?

            

              
              
              
            

            
The cost grows one-for-one with the list. What's a handful of calls on the demo catalog is hundreds on a real one, each with its own round trip and its own load on the downstream service, so the page gets slower exactly as the catalog grows.

          

          

            See it in New Relic
            
Each call is cheap; the count is the problem

            
Any single product call is quick. The damage is the sheer number of them, which is why the fix is to make fewer calls, not faster ones.

Step 6 · The fix

            
How do you fix an N+1?

            

              
              
              
            

            
Collapse the N calls into one: a single batched request, a join, or an "in" query that returns every item at once. The row of identical spans becomes one span, and the page stops slowing down as the catalog grows.

          

          

            The takeaway
            
Make fewer calls, not faster ones

            
Throwing hardware or retries at an N+1 treats the symptom. The fix is to stop fanning out: ask for everything in one request.

          

        


      


      
      

        
Case closed

        
The catalog asked for one thing at a time.

        
The page was slow and the product service was swamped because loading the catalog made one call to list the items and then one more call for every item, the N+1 pattern. One request fanned out into N+1. The trace made it obvious: a single catalog span over a stack of identical product calls.

        
What you'd do next in the real world:

        
    
          
  • Replace the per-item calls with one batched request (a join, an "in" query, or a bulk endpoint that takes all the ids).
    • 
          
  • Re-open the trace and confirm the row of spans has collapsed to one.
    • 
          
  • Watch for the pattern in code review and in slow-trace samples: a loop that makes a call each time around is the usual source.
    • 
        

        
Why it worked: the waterfall shows the shape of a request. N+1 has a shape, a run of identical spans, that you can spot without reading a line of code.
